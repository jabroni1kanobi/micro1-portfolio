01 · Melee Coach — live ranked coaching, and the Reaction Sheet

A coach that runs off real match telemetry: replay in, a per-player stats sheet out, roughly forty evidence-backed habit triggers, a character bank plus personal baselines, then a twenty-second card in the intermission and a full recap after the set. It is post-set only — simulation, inputs, rollback, ELO and the queue are untouched, so competitive integrity is a design constraint, not a promise.

The build, working live

COACH BUILD — ACTUAL FOOTAGE. The coach running in the real build: telemetry in, a coaching card out, in real time, with the logic reading the match rather than guessing at it.

The Reaction Sheet — same game, more feelings

REACTION SHEET v1.0 — 26 characters × 4 reactions, two animation frames each. The point is not the portraits: it is that the coach can speak in the fighter's own voice. A teardown is not an abstract statistic, it is the character looking at you. Every reaction is chosen by the trigger that fired, so the sheet is the coaching feedback made legible at a glance — click to zoom.
telemetry→stats sheet→~40 habit triggers→character bank + personal baselines→peer review→Reaction Sheet pick→20-second card + messenger

How the reactions are picked

  • Legibility: a player reads a face, not a JSON payload
  • Voice: the feedback belongs to the game, not to a dashboard
  • Coverage: 104 reactions give the 40 triggers somewhere to land
  • Every trigger ships with scripted fixtures and exact expected output

02 · Chao Buddy Garden — one creature, two loops, 832 quests

A persistent 3D garden running the real Sonic Adventure 2 Chao runtime, not a reimplementation. Two loops raise the same creature: the work you finish and the matches you play. Fighters you play push their own affinity into the creature; the kind of work you do pushes the rest. Eighty-two quests at the top level of that, or eight hundred and thirty-two if you count the tiers.

The design plan, every slide — J-Dex WorkBuddy Garden

This is the document I wrote before the build existed: ten slides that take a reward engine from a desktop productivity app into a 3D game world, and then close the loop between the two. Click any slide to zoom.

01 · WorkBuddy Garden

A tiny Chao Garden companion built into J-Dex. Native popup window, no visible avatar, mouse-native interaction, full 3D camera control, and a garden that lives from any angle.

02 · Mouse-native controls

No visible avatar: just the cursor, the camera and the garden. Point, interact, pick up, flick, release, and scroll or use dedicated buttons to zoom in and out.

03 · Garden HUD and daily care

Pet the Chao, pick them up, feed them, place items. Live rings, happiness, streak, energy, mood and adventure state, and a garden that grows with you.

04 · Rewards from real work

Coding progress becomes rings, gifts and slow-burn Chao growth. Implement user authentication, streak days, refer-a-friend, memory leak or renderer bugs, and the reward is tied to the thing you actually finished. Plus low-effort spam gates.

05 · Shop and inventory economy

Everything priced in rings, every purchase inside the game where progress is visible. A ring economy with real sinks, an expensive rare tier, and a deliberately limited quick-sale-buy path.

06 · Contests and long-term progression

Raise a Chao over days of real work, then prove it in contests. Care for Chao, build strength, technique and focus, then earn trophies, titles, unique accessories and new garden areas.

07 · Hero Garden unlock

The path of good after earning it: four milestones from Neutral Garden to Hero Garden, each with its own win condition, rewards and unique interactions.

08 · Dark Garden unlock

The second alignment path, with its own long-term payoff. A darker Chao form, new mechanics and tricks, different experiences — and a richer potential pool behind a locked area.

09 · J-Dex to Garden reward flow

The whole loop in four panels: prompt, work completed, reward granted, garden progress. This is the exact chain the shipped system runs.

10 · The full WorkBuddy loop

Six stages in one view — do real work, earn rings and progress, shop for items, raise the Chao, enter races and contests, unlock new gardens and karma forms. A more productive, brighter tomorrow.

Why it is written this way

  • Observable, not implied. Every reward names the task that caused it, so the player can always answer "why did I get that?"
  • Spam gates built into the loop, not bolted on — low-effort prompts pay less by design.
  • Two alignment paths so the long game has a choice, not just a longer list.
  • Visible growth. Rings, trophies, accessories and new areas, all in the game where the work happened.

And where it came from

"A more playful, personal side of productive work." — the thesis the whole plan is built on, and the reason the same reward engine ended up inside a game.

03 · Bloodborne on YouGame — full port, live online, plus a Hardcore covenant economy

The open-source Bloodborne PC port (bbport, GPL-2.0) running inside YouGame's sandboxed desktop app, tested — including online — with the game logic untouched. Servers replaced, not the client: an in-process gateway answers the game's own shadNet, game-server and peer-to-peer sockets on loopback and routes everything to YouGame. Players bring their own legally obtained copy; no game data is ever shipped, hosted or linked.

The port

  • ~15-line loader plus configuration the port already reads; the store ships updates so the port's own updater stays off
  • Game code unmodified — servers replaced, not the client
  • Manifest-verified player-owned dump: 551 of 28,844 files read at boot, zero written
  • Error 487 diagnosed and fixed by measurement: reserve the window around where Windows placed it, cap allocations at 1 TiB

Online systems, live

  • Accounts, identity, friends, directed-summon invites — bells still get rung, Bloodborne's rule survives
  • Matchmaking rooms, summon and invasion broker, relay-or-direct peer-to-peer
  • Cloud saves landing about a second after the last write of a burst
  • Server Lite community world: notes, bloodstains, ghosts, glyphs, lamps, messenger
  • Optional Seamless Co-op: 55 of 55 byte patches, 159 hooks installed, the game continuing into the Hunter's Dream

Hardcore — the covenant economy I designed on top

A whole second game layered on the same world, so it crosses co-op, invasions and Seamless Co-op at once. You opt in and become a hardcore hunter: die and you lose your hunter and everything you are carrying, and the rest drops for the hunters around you to take. Everything below is designed and built, and it crosses systems rather than sitting in one menu.

Two covenants

Evil and Good, each with 50 titles, a badge per title, and the quests that unlock them. Alignment is earned, not chosen, and rank inside your covenant is public.

Bounties & escrow

Stake items on another hunter. The stake is escrowed and stays hidden until the game's own receipts confirm what was actually lost — a bounty pays out on evidence, not on a claim.

Progression that costs something

Kill credit, titles and badges all run through the same receipt path, so a win has to be real. Two hunters sharing a co-op room no longer credit each other with kills.

Loud, visible rewards

Flashy profile icons, an aura that reads your standing at a glance, and a public leaderboard across all covenants — the reward is meant to be seen.

  • Board and leaderboard ship in-game: bounties, leaderboard, titles and badges, relics, a live death log with causes, and your own state — opened from inside the game itself
  • Hardcore mode is a switch on the listing page; the game reads the choice at launch and keeps every other key in your account slot

How it was tested

  • Gates BG1, BG3, BG4, BG5, BG7, BG8 green; BG2 blocked honestly on memory (about 8 GB per instance)
  • 172 world-module tests, gateway unit and end-to-end suites, hostile-input and fuzz guards
  • Scripted co-op scenarios S1–S10: sign-in, traditional and seamless co-op, invasion, password rooms, session end, messaging, stains and ghosts, chalice, directed invite
  • A private tester guide whose on-platform self-test ends in an explicit WORLD CHECK PASS / FAIL

What I proved does not fit

Whole-game rollback measured at about 61.5 ms of single-core CPU per 30 fps frame across 103 host threads — 7.4× too slow for the three frames a rollback needs. So co-op rides the game's own netcode with relay batching, packet priority and a direct path beside it. Measuring the no is part of the job.

06 · Design philosophy — a social layer for competitive games

Eight slides, click any one to zoom. The argument: competitive depth keeps a game worth mastering, but belonging and recognition keep people logging in. Casuals churn on win-or-nothing; they stay for identity, shared goals, visible creation and team play. Halo 2's clans and Halo 3's creation economy are the precedents, and a human editorial voice closes the loop. This is also the argument underneath the coach's Reaction Sheet and the Hardcore covenants: rewards have to be seen, and they have to be social.

07 · Where these systems came from

These are not three unrelated projects. They are one idea I have been carrying since I was a kid, built three times as the tools to build it got better.

  1. The childhood version. Before any of this was a job, the thing I wanted to play was a game where the game noticed you — where effort left a mark on the world and other people could see it. Not a scoreboard. A relationship.
  2. J-Dex: the first build. When I started AI coding, I saw what tools like Codex were doing with companion features and it struck me as obvious that they had stopped one layer short. A coding app that knows you and pays you back for real work would be more fun and more rewarding than one that just answers. So I started with the coding layer, because that was the layer I actually had.
  3. The problem with stopping there. A reward loop bolted onto a productivity tool only reaches the people already in that tool. The moment I saw it work, the obvious conclusion was that none of this was specific to coding — it belonged to any game. So the next question was which game.
  4. Chao Buddy: the system moved into a game. A creature that grows from real work and real matches, 832 quests, a shop, gardens you unlock by caring for something properly. The J-Dex reward engine was the prototype; the Chao runtime was the host. Rewards that are visible, slow, and earned in real space instead of a dropdown.
  5. Melee Coach: the same instinct, aimed at skill. The Reaction Sheet is the same idea turned inward. Instead of a reward for doing work, a reaction that speaks the fighter's language back at you the moment you do something worth noticing. Legibility as care.
  6. Bloodborne Hardcore: the same idea, with stakes. Covenants, titles, badges, bounties escrowed against real receipts — a social layer built so that what you carry and what you are worth are visible to other players. Alignment, rank, and a death that costs something.
  7. Why I build this way. Every one of those systems answers the same design question: what does the game give back, when, and how does the player know they got it. Depth is cheap to add. Belonging is the part that has to be designed.

09 · Ultimelee — an 89-fighter cast into a 2001 fighting-game engine

A fan engineering project and my largest to date: bringing the full 89-fighter cast of a modern platform fighter into a native PC port of a 2001 fighting-game engine whose source we can edit. Nothing is hand-ported fighter by fighter. A custom toolchain, the Foundry, captures what each move does in the source game and generates engine code from that data, then proves it in headless matches.

Where it honestly stands

  • Working today: all 89 fighters load and fight in headless CPU-vs-CPU matches.
  • Work in progress: move-by-move parity with the source game — specials, articles (projectiles and held items) and effects. It is tracked per fighter and per part on a progress board, and a move only counts as done when a real headless match proves it frame by frame.
  • Not claimed: this is not finished, and I do not report it as nearly finished. Entry gates (“it loads”) are not parity, and I stopped reporting them as if they were.

Capture pipeline

  • Records what every move does in the source game: frame data, hitboxes, values, articles, effects and sounds, from game data first and from instrumented traces of the running game for numbers static files do not hold.
  • An oracle runner replays scenarios in the reference game with frozen inputs so a capture is made once and reused, not re-measured.

Compiler from captured data to engine code

  • One rule per move family, never per fighter: a detector, a parameterized emitter and a registry row, then a regeneration sweep over all 89 fighters.
  • Gaps are named and refused, never filled with guessed values; every fix also leaves a regression test.

Animation and skeletons

  • Skeleton conversion between the two games' rigs and animation conversion with interpolation, so motion keeps its timing in the older engine.

Models and effects

  • Model import and an effects pipeline that converts the source game's effect files into flipbook-style effects the old engine can play.

Engine work: systems the old engine never had

  • Articles and projectiles built as engine items, following one hand-built reference fighter as the pattern.
  • Fighters that transform or swap, such as paired characters, sharing one slot through the engine's own transform and partner code.

Headless testing and gates

  • Automated headless matches drive each move and compare it frame by frame against the capture.
  • Gates are themselves tested: a gate must show it can pass known-good work and fail injected errors before its verdicts are believed. Self-tests found false failures in my own early gates, which were then fixed as bugs rather than loosened.

Netcode

  • Rollback netcode is kept in sync as a standing requirement, checked as part of the definition of done rather than at the end.

Orchestration

  • A work queue with claims, a parts board that ranks the causes blocking progress, and guards that block the worst mistakes automatically, so several agents can work in parallel without overlapping.
  • Cheap tools and models take the mechanical work; the expensive ones direct.
